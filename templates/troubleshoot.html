Troubleshooting

Something went wrong. Let's fix it.

Every baker has a disaster loaf. Most bakers have several. The ones who improve are the ones who figure out why, rather than blaming the flour and never speaking of it again. Find your symptom below and start from the most likely cause.

Bread didn't rise

Completely flat loaf with no oven spring

Most likely causes:

  • Starter wasn't active. If it didn't pass the float test and hadn't visibly doubled before use, it wasn't ready. Feed it, wait for peak activity, then bake.
  • Salt killed the starter. Adding salt directly onto the starter before mixing it through the dough can inhibit yeast. Always disperse the starter into the dough first, then add salt on top.
  • Over-proofed before baking. If the dough rose beautifully during bulk and then went flat in the oven, fermentation was exhausted. The gluten had nothing left to hold gas. Move it earlier next time.

Try differently: Float test your starter before committing. Cold dough = firm, springy bake. Warm, wobbly dough after a long proof = get it in immediately or you will lose it.

Spread sideways instead of rising up

Most likely causes:

  • Dough too wet for your skill level. High-hydration dough needs strong gluten and confident shaping. At 78%+, any weakness in structure becomes a puddle.
  • Gluten under-developed. Too few stretch-and-folds, or skipped entirely. The gluten network couldn't hold the gas and the structure collapsed outward.
  • Shaped too loosely. Shaping is about surface tension. If the shaped loaf felt soft and puffy rather than taut like a drum skin, it hadn't enough structure to hold up.

Try differently: Drop hydration to 70-72%. Do all four sets of stretch-and-folds. When shaping, work for genuine surface tension: you should feel resistance when you drag the dough toward you on the bench.

Rose in the oven, then collapsed

Most likely causes:

  • Over-proofed. The dough rose fully during fermentation, then the oven heat couldn't set the structure before it fell in on itself. Gas escaped faster than the crumb set.
  • Gluten exhausted. Long, warm fermentation degrades gluten via protease enzyme activity. The structure simply wasn't strong enough to hold after the initial spring.

Try differently: Shorten bulk by 30 minutes. Use a cooler proofing spot or go straight to a cold retard in the fridge. Bake with a firm, cold dough for best structural results.

Crumb problems

Dense, tight crumb with almost no holes

Most likely causes:

  • Under-proofed. The most common culprit. The dough didn't have enough time to ferment fully. Fermentation builds the gas; you cannot shortcut it.
  • Starter sluggish. If the starter was past peak, or hadn't been fed recently, fermentation stalled and the dough never got going properly.
  • Dough too stiff. Very low hydration leaves less room for gas bubbles to form and expand. Even a small increase of 3-5% can noticeably open the crumb.

Try differently: Let the bulk go longer. Look for the 50-75% volume increase and the jiggle test before shaping, not the clock. Freshen your starter the night before.

Gummy, wet line near the bottom

Most likely causes:

  • Undercooked. The most reliable fix: check internal temperature. It needs to reach 96°C / 205°F. Pull it earlier and it will be gummy every time.
  • Cut too early. The crumb needs at least one hour to set after baking (90 minutes for freestanding loaves). Steam inside is still doing its job. Cutting early produces a gummy crumb, every single time.
  • Too much water. Very high hydration can leave excess moisture that concentrates at the base if the bake was not long enough.

Try differently: Use a probe thermometer. 96°C / 205°F is the number you want. Bake 5 minutes longer. Then wait: the bread is not ready just because it is out of the oven.

Huge random holes with dense patches elsewhere

Most likely causes:

  • Flour pockets not incorporated. Dry flour left in the dough creates weak spots that collapse into large holes while the rest stays tight. Mix more thoroughly at the start.
  • Over-proofed sections. If the dough proofed unevenly (warmer spot in the bowl), some areas over-fermented while others didn't. Keep the dough at an even temperature.

Try differently: Mix until you see no dry streaks whatsoever. Cover the bowl and keep it away from draughts and direct heat sources.

No ear: the score didn't open

Most likely causes:

  • Scored too shallow. A timid score barely scratches the surface. The skin seals before the bread can spring. Go at least 1cm deep with confidence.
  • Scored at the wrong angle. Perpendicular scoring seals itself shut. Score at 30-45 degrees to the surface: that angle is what creates the flap that lifts into an ear.
  • Dough too warm going into the oven. Warm dough loses shape quickly once scored. The ear needs surface tension that cold, firm dough provides. Bake from cold.

Try differently: Score with a very sharp lame or a new razor blade at a 45-degree angle, one confident stroke. Bake straight from fridge.

Flavour problems

Tastes flat or bland

Most likely causes:

  • Not enough fermentation time. Flavour compounds build up over time. A quick 2-hour bulk at high temperature produces almost no flavour complexity. Slow it down.
  • Salt was forgotten. Salt does considerably more than season: it suppresses competing bacteria and helps balance fermentation. Without it, the bread tastes flat and oddly sweet.
  • Starter not mature enough. A young starter (under 2 weeks old) produces inconsistent and often mild results. Keep feeding it. It gets better.

Try differently: Add an overnight cold retard. Lower your starter percentage to 6-8% and give it more time. Your dough before baking should smell faintly tangy and yeasty, not neutral.

Too sour: sharp and almost vinegar-like

Most likely causes:

  • Fermented too long. The longer and cooler the fermentation, the more acetic acid (the sharp, vinegary one) builds up relative to lactic acid (the mild, creamy one).
  • Very long cold proof. 24-36 hours in the fridge, particularly with a high rye content, will produce an assertively sour loaf. That may well be what you wanted. If not, shorten the retard.
  • High rye content. Rye naturally drives more acetic acid production. Blending in 20-30% rye is fine. Going much higher shifts the flavour profile quite dramatically.

Try differently: Shorten bulk, warm your proofing spot to 25°C, cut the cold retard to 8-10 hours, and keep rye at 20% or below.

Not sour at all: tastes like regular bread

Most likely causes:

  • Short, warm fermentation favours lactic acid. Warm temperatures and high hydration produce a mild, yoghurt-y lactic profile. This is not a failure. It is chemistry working exactly as it should, just not in the direction you wanted.
  • High starter percentage. Using 15-20% starter speeds things up and produces a mild loaf. Drop to 6-8% and give it considerably more time.

Try differently: Lower your room temperature to 19-21°C, reduce starter to 6-8%, and add a long cold retard of 12+ hours. That combination pushes the flavour firmly into acetic territory.

Yeasty or alcoholic taste

Most likely causes:

  • Over-fermented. Ethanol builds up as yeast exhaust the available sugars and the dough ferments past its peak. The bread smells and tastes boozy and stale.
  • Too warm for too long. A warm kitchen (25°C+) combined with a high starter percentage can push bulk fermentation into over-territory in as little as 3–4 hours.

Try differently: Bake earlier in bulk: look for a 50% rise rather than a 75% one. Move the dough somewhere cooler, or reduce the starter percentage significantly.

Crust problems

Pale, soft crust that goes soggy

Most likely causes:

  • Oven not hot enough. Sourdough crust needs serious heat: 230°C / 450°F minimum. Many home ovens run 15-20°C cooler than the dial claims. An oven thermometer is one of the most useful purchases you can make.
  • Dutch oven not preheated long enough. It needs a full 60 minutes at temperature. A cold or lukewarm Dutch oven traps moisture instead of blasting it off.
  • Too much steam in the second half. If you kept the lid on too long (over 25 minutes), excess steam softened the crust permanently. Remove the lid on time.

Try differently: Preheat the full 60 minutes. Use an oven thermometer and add 10°C if yours runs cold. Remove the lid at exactly 20 minutes.

Thick, tooth-breaking crust

Most likely causes:

  • Overbaked. The crust thickens and hardens the longer it is in the oven beyond 96°C internal. Reduce baking time or temperature.
  • Not enough steam in the first 20 minutes. The Dutch oven lid is what provides the initial steam. A loose-fitting lid, or lifting it too early, reduces steam and thickens the crust.

Try differently: Check internal temp and pull the bread earlier. Make sure your Dutch oven lid fits snugly. A small piece of foil pushed into the gap helps if yours is loose.

Burns on the bottom

Most likely causes:

  • Rack too low. Move it one notch up. The bottom element in most ovens is the primary heat source and will scorch the base if the bread sits too close.
  • Dutch oven too close to element. Same fix. Or place a second, empty baking sheet on the shelf below the Dutch oven to buffer the direct heat.
  • Dark-coloured Dutch oven. Dark finishes absorb more heat. Reduce temperature by 10–15°C if you bake in a dark cast iron pot.

Try differently: Middle rack, always. Add a buffer tray below if bottom scorching persists. Check the base colour at the 30-minute mark (when you remove the lid) and react if it's already dark.
