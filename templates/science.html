The Science

Why sourdough works

Sourdough is not magic. It is a small, teeming city of microorganisms working in surprisingly well-organised chaos. Understanding the biology will not just make you a better baker; it will make every loaf feel like a minor miracle you actually understand.

Enzymes: the invisible prep cooks

Long before your yeast and bacteria even notice there is work to be done, two enzymes have already rolled up their sleeves. They activate the moment flour meets water, asking for no instructions and requiring no supervision.

Amylase

Amylase chops starches into simpler sugars, mainly maltose. This matters for two reasons: yeast eat those sugars to produce CO2 (your lift), and during baking those same sugars drive the Maillard reaction, which is responsible for your crust's deep amber colour and the complex toasty flavour that makes the kitchen smell incredible.

Wholegrain and rye flours have high amylase activity. Too much and the starch structure weakens, giving you a gummy crumb. This is largely why rye behaves so differently from white wheat flour.

Protease

Protease snips the protein chains (glutenin and gliadin) that form gluten. A modest amount of this makes dough wonderfully extensible, meaning you can stretch it without it snapping back at you. Autolyse (resting flour and water before adding starter) exists specifically to give protease this quiet window.

Leave the dough too long in a warm place and protease goes too far, degrading gluten completely. The dough becomes a puddle. Ferment with intention, not ambition.

Autolyse, demystified: Mix flour and water, wait 20-60 minutes, then add starter and salt. In that window, amylase builds sugar reserves and protease softens the gluten network. The result is a noticeably silkier dough that requires far less muscle to develop. It is not a trend; it is chemistry.

Yeast: the leavening engine

Yeast are saprotrophic fungi, meaning they earn a living by breaking down organic matter. In your dough, that means sugars. The dominant species in most starters is Saccharomyces cerevisiae, which is also responsible for beer, wine, and commercial bread yeast. Wild starters often host Kazachstania humilis alongside it, which is more acid-tolerant and partly explains why sourdough starters survive conditions that would kill commercial yeast outright.

Two modes, one organism

Yeast switch their metabolism depending on how much oxygen is available:

  • With oxygen (aerobic): yeast reproduce quickly, consuming sugar to produce CO2 and water. Minimal alcohol. This phase dominates early in the mix, while the dough still has trapped air.
  • Without oxygen (anaerobic): as the dough tightens and air is expelled, yeast switch to fermentation. Now they produce CO2 and ethanol. The CO2 inflates your gluten bubbles. The ethanol evaporates during baking and contributes to crust flavour.

Temperature sensitivity

Yeast activity roughly doubles for every 6-7°C increase between 4°C and 38°C. Below 4°C, they are functionally asleep. Above 38°C, they start dying. This is why cold-proofing slows everything down, and why a warm kitchen can turn a 6-hour bulk into a 3-hour panic.

Why cold proofing works: At 4-8°C, yeast activity drops to almost nothing. Bacteria, however, keep quietly working. The extended, cold fermentation shifts flavour production towards acetic acid (more tang), and the cold dough holds its shape brilliantly under the score. Bake straight from cold. Warming it up first is a common and avoidable mistake.

Bacteria: the flavour makers

Here is the fact that surprises almost everyone: a healthy sourdough starter contains roughly 100 bacteria for every single yeast cell. The bacteria are running this show. Yeast provide the lift, but bacteria provide the character.

The dominant bacteria are lactic acid bacteria (LAB) of the Lactobacillus genus. They are the same group responsible for yoghurt, kimchi, and cheese. They thrive in acidic, low-oxygen environments, tolerate salt, and are not easily crowded out by competitors. They have been doing this for millennia and they are very good at it.

Homofermentative LAB

These produce only lactic acid from glucose. Efficient and clean. They dominate at warmer temperatures (28-35°C) and higher hydrations.

Result: a mild, rounded sourness with creamy, yoghurt-like notes. Pleasant and accessible. Good for feeding people who claim they do not like sour bread.

Heterofermentative LAB

These produce lactic acid plus acetic acid and CO2. More complex metabolically. They dominate at cooler temperatures (18-24°C) and lower hydrations.

Result: a sharper, more assertive tang with vinegary top notes. The flavour profile most people picture when they say "proper sourdough".

Lactic vs. acetic acid

Both acids keep the bread from going off quickly, and both contribute to flavour. But they taste completely different and respond to completely different conditions. As a baker, this is one of the most useful levers you have.

Lactic acid Acetic acid
Flavour Mild, creamy, yoghurt-y Sharp, tangy, vinegar-forward
Favoured by Warm temps, high hydration, shorter bulk Cool temps, lower hydration, long or cold bulk
Crumb character More open, lighter crust colour Denser, deeper, more chewy crust
Classic example French pain au levain San Francisco sourdough
The baker's lever: You control the balance through temperature and timing. A warm bulk at 26°C followed by a short cold proof produces a lactic, approachable loaf. A cooler bulk at 19-21°C followed by a long overnight retard pushes you firmly into acetic territory. Neither is better. They are different breads.

Temperature and fermentation speed

Temperature is the single most powerful variable available to a home baker, and also the most commonly ignored. The Q10 rule describes how biological reaction rates change with temperature: broadly, fermentation roughly doubles for every 10°C increase, or halves for every 10°C drop. For sourdough, the practical doubling point is around 6-7°C.

Desired Dough Temperature (DDT)

Professional bakers do not guess the water temperature. They calculate it. The DDT formula ensures every mix lands at the same starting temperature regardless of ambient conditions:

Water temp = (DDT x 4) - flour temp - room temp - levain temp - friction factor

The multiplier of 4 accounts for the four temperature inputs: room, flour, levain, and water. Friction factor is the heat added by mechanical mixing: roughly 0 by hand, 10-25°C for a stand mixer on medium. A bit of maths before mixing saves you a lot of guesswork during bulk.

Baker's maths

Baker's percentage expresses every ingredient as a proportion of total flour weight. Flour is always 100%, regardless of actual grams. This means you can compare recipes of different sizes directly, scale any recipe without a calculator, and immediately understand a recipe's character just by glancing at the numbers.

Accounting for starter hydration

A starter is itself a mixture of flour and water. To calculate your actual dough hydration correctly, you need to split the starter into its component parts. The formula:

Water from starter = starter weight x (hydration / (1 + hydration))

For a 100% hydration starter (equal parts flour and water by weight): 50g of starter contributes 25g of flour and 25g of water. Bread Buddy uses this formula internally so the hydration percentage it shows you is always honest.

What salt actually does

Salt is not just seasoning. It tightens the gluten network (making the dough stronger and better at holding gas), slows fermentation (giving you more control over your schedule), and suppresses wild bacteria that would otherwise compete with your LAB. The sweet spot is 1.8-2.2%. Below 1.5% and your bread will taste flat and ferment unpredictably. Above 2.5% and the dough is noticeably salty and fermentation drags.

A short history

Sourdough is the oldest form of leavened bread by a considerable margin. Archaeological evidence points to fermented grain breads in ancient Egypt around 3000 BCE, almost certainly discovered when some dough was left sitting in warm conditions long enough for wild yeasts to take hold. As origin stories go, it is a good one.

For most of recorded history, the starter was the only leavening available. Bakers maintained their cultures across decades and generations, and a working starter was a genuinely valuable thing to own. The Industrial Revolution changed everything: commercial yeast (reliably isolated Saccharomyces cerevisiae) became commercially available in the mid-1800s, offering fast, predictable results without the fuss of maintaining a live culture. By the mid-20th century, industrial bread had almost completely replaced it.

The revival began quietly in the 1970s and gathered serious momentum in the 2010s, driven by a growing interest in fermentation, flavour complexity, and food that lasts longer without a list of preservatives on the label. The COVID-19 lockdowns of 2020 produced a global sourdough moment. The flour shortage that followed confirmed that a great many people were taking it seriously.

Today the science is well-documented, the community is enormous, and the tools are better than they have ever been. You are holding one of them.
